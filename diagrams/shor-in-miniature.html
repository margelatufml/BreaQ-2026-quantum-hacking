Shor in Miniature

The quantum circuit does one job: it finds how often the powers of a number repeat (the period). Ordinary arithmetic turns that period into the factors. Part B scales the same idea up to RSA-2048 and asks how far today's machines are.

The project at a glance

  1. classicalPre-checks21 is odd and not a power; pick a = 2; gcd(2, 21) = 1.
  2. quantumBuild the circuitbuild_shor(21, 2): 15 qubits, never told the answer.
  3. quantumRun 20 shots20 clues y, mostly near multiples of 1024/6.
  4. classicalFind the periodFractions and LCM give r = 6, since 2⁶ mod 21 = 1.
  5. outputFactorsx = 2³ mod 21 = 8; gcd(7, 21), gcd(9, 21) give (3, 7).

What the judges do

They call build_shor(N, a) and factor(N) on hidden odd composite numbers up to 6 bits, and run two tests per case:

  1. Distribution: the TV distance between our 2,000-shot histogram and the ideal one must be below 0.1.
  2. Factoring: factor(N) returns the right factors in time, using only our circuit.

Forbidden ("compiled Shor"): giving the circuit r or the factors, looking N up in a table, or finding r classically inside factor. Precomputing a^(2^j) mod N is allowed.

  2. quantum

    Build the circuit: build_shor(21, 2)

    IN
    only N = 21 and a = 2; the period r is never given
    DO
    10 counting qubits try every x from 0 to 1023 at once; 5 work qubits compute 2ˣ mod 21 for all of them; an inverse QFT turns the repeat into peaks; measure the counting qubits.
    OUT
    a 15-qubit circuit, not run yet (section 2 opens it up)

    Why: 2ˣ mod 21 repeats every 6 steps (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 11, 1, …). The circuit hides that 6 in the counting qubits, where the inverse QFT can turn it into readable peaks.

    n = N.bit_length()        # 5
t = 2 * n                 # 10
qc.x(work[0])             # calculator = 1
qc.h(cnt)                 # all x at once
consts = mult_constants(N, a, t)
for q in range(t):
    qc.append(_ctrl_perm_gate(N, n, consts[t - 1 - q]),
              [cnt[q]] + list(work))
qc.compose(iqft_circuit(t), qubits=list(cnt), inplace=True)
qc.measure(cnt, c)

    15 qubits · 1 X · 20 H · 10 controlled multiplications · 45 controlled phases · 10 measurements

    IN

    OUT
    20 clues y

    Why: one run gives one random clue, and one clue often gives only part of r, so we collect several. Most clues sit at the peaks; a few (352, 837) land in the small tails, because 1024/6 is not a whole number.

  5. check

    Bottom numbers to the period r

    IN
    the bottom numbers, in shot order
    DO
    Combine them with the least common multiple (LCM) until 2ʳ mod 21 = 1. Then try to shrink r (would 3 or 2 also work?), and check that r explains at least half of all clues.
    OUT

    Why: the LCM glues pieces such as 2 and 3 into 6. 2ʳ mod 21 = 1 is what "period" means. The half-of-the-clues check is the anti-cheat: random numbers could pass the first test, but they don't explain most clues.

  6. check

    Is r useful?

    IN
    r = 6
    DO
    Check that r is even, compute x = 2^(6/2) mod 21, and check that x is not 20 (−1 mod 21).
    OUT
    x = 8

    Why: x² = 2⁶ leaves remainder 1, so (x − 1)(x + 1) is a multiple of 21 and the factors hide in x − 1 and x + 1. An odd r can't be halved (a = 4 gives r = 3), and x = −1 gives only 1 and 21 (a = 5).

  7. output

    The factors

    IN
    x = 8
    DO
    gcd(8 − 1, 21) and gcd(8 + 1, 21); keep a result strictly between 1 and 21.
    OUT
    factor(21) = (3, 7)

    Why: 7 × 9 = 63 = 3 × 21, so 21's factors are split between 7 and 9, and the gcd pulls out the shared part.

3 · Why each choice

Why 2n counting qubits

With 2¹⁰ = 1,024 positions for numbers below 21, two different fractions s/r can't land on the same clue, so continued fractions return the right bottom number. With fewer qubits they can return the wrong one; the brief gives this as a hint.

Why combine several clues

A clue like 512 → 1/2 gives only 2, and 683 → 2/3 gives only 3, because the fraction simplified. The LCM of the pieces gives the full period, 6.

Why the "half of the clues" check stays

Turned off, factor "factored" pure random numbers in 70 of 70 runs, with no circuit at all, because the LCM of many random bottom numbers eventually contains the period. With it on: 0 of 100. It keeps the brief's rule that factoring uses only our circuit.

Why some clues are off-peak

1024/6 = 170.67 is not a whole number, so each peak leaks a little onto nearby values. Clues like 352 → 7/20 don't fit; the check sees 18 of 20 fit, which is fine.

Why it sometimes retries

Shor's trick fails for some a, for classical reasons: an odd period (a = 4 gives r = 3) or x = −1 (a = 5: 5³ mod 21 = 20). factor simply picks a new random a.

Why the judges also test the histogram

The final gcd step can turn even random numbers into factors now and then, so checking only the answer isn't enough. A TV below 0.1 shows the circuit itself is right. Our Level 1 matches the ideal exactly (TV ≤ 2.3 × 10⁻¹³ on the statevector).

4 · What we simplified

The code is the design the brief prescribes. After cleanup, shor.py went from 321 to 300 lines and the full test suite still passes.

Removed

  • transpile before running: the simulator understands all our gates, and the shots were identical without it.
  • The build_shor(level=…) switch: call build_shor_level2 or build_shor_semiclassical directly.
  • 4 alternative function names that guessed at the judges' harness.
  • prime_factors: not in the brief; factor returns one split (p, q).

Kept, because each one protects points

  • Level 1, Level 2 and both bonus variants (levels and the qubit tie-breaker).
  • Perfect-power and gcd checks (the brief's common mistakes).
  • Shrinking r (an off-peak clue can make the LCM too big).
  • The half-of-the-clues check (otherwise random numbers "factor").
  • noise_table.py: evidence that even a perfect circuit can fail TV < 0.1 by chance.

Test results (final run)

  • Level 1: every 4-, 5- and 6-bit odd composite and every a: the exact distribution equals the ideal.
  • 2,000 shots: TV < 0.1 in 63 of 64 cases; N = 27, a = 23 gives 0.1008 from shot noise alone.
  • Level 2: (15, 7) TV 0.012, (21, 2) TV 0.045 at 2,000 shots.
  • factor(N) is correct for every odd composite up to 63; random numbers succeed 0 of 100 times.

The noise risk to raise with a mentor

A perfect circuit can still fail the judges' TV test by chance when the period is long: up to 8.7% of runs for N = 25, up to 4% for N = 27, and every run for N = 49 with r = 42. That is shot noise, not a bug, and it affects every team.

5 · Part B: how far is RSA-2048?

The question: how many good physical qubits would a real attack need, and how far are today's machines? Our own arithmetic uses the brief's toy surface-code model.

  1. assume

    Physical error rate p = 0.1%

    IN
    p = 0.001 per gate, threshold 1%
    DO
    Error correction groups many physical qubits into one logical qubit; a larger code distance d lowers its error.
    OUT
    the logical error per qubit per cycle, p_L

    Why: today's qubits fail far too often for a days-long computation, so they must be protected.

    p_L = 0.1 · (100p)^((d+1)/2)

  2. workload

    1,500 logical qubits for 5 days

    IN
    the judges' worked example, at a 1 µs cycle
    DO
    Count error-correction cycles.
    OUT
    T cycles

    T = 5 · 86,400 s / 1 µs
    = 4.32 × 10¹¹ cycles

  3. check

    Need L · T · p_L < 0.1

    IN
    L = 1,500, T = 4.32 × 10¹¹
    DO
    Find the smallest odd d whose total failure chance is below 10%.
    OUT
    d = 29

    Why: less than a 10% chance that any logical qubit fails during the whole run.

    p_L < 1.54 × 10⁻¹⁶
    smallest odd d = 29

  4. result

    About 2.52 million physical qubits

    IN
    d = 29, L = 1,500
    DO
    2d² physical qubits per logical qubit, times L.
    OUT
    2,523,000, before magic-state factories

    Why: lands in the 10⁶–10⁷ range the brief expects; Gidney's tricks bring the real figure under one million.

    1,500 × 1,682 = 2,523,000

The gap today

  • Helios: 98 qubits with two-qubit error 7.9 × 10⁻⁴, already better than the assumed 10⁻³. Gap to Gidney 2025: 9,162× in qubits.
  • The largest verified array has 6,100 atomic qubits, still 147× short, and without gate errors shown at that scale.
  • The 20× drop from 2019 to 2025 came from algorithms (yoked surface codes, magic state cultivation, approximate residue arithmetic), largely by trading qubits for runtime.

Timeline and consequence

  • Depending on how fast hardware and algorithms improve, the crossing lands between 2033 and 2065.
  • Mosca's rule: if secrecy time plus migration time exceeds the time to a machine, you are already late.
  • Recorded traffic can be decrypted later, so key exchange should move to ML-KEM now.

6 · Words you need

Factor
A number that divides N exactly: 3 and 7 for 21.
gcd
Greatest common divisor: the biggest number dividing both. gcd(9, 21) = 3.
Coprime
gcd = 1: the two numbers share no factor.
mod
The remainder after dividing: 64 mod 21 = 1.
Period (order) r
How many multiplications by a bring you back to 1: 6 for a = 2, N = 21.
Counting register
The 2n qubits that hold every x, and at the end the clue y.
Work register
The n qubits acting as a calculator for aˣ mod N.
Superposition
A qubit holding a mix of 0 and 1; 10 of them hold all 1,024 values of x at once.
Controlled gate
A gate that acts only when its control qubit is 1.
Permutation matrix
A 0/1 lookup table written as a matrix; it can be run backwards.
Inverse QFT
Turns a repeating pattern into peaks, like an FFT finds a rhythm.
Shot
One run of the circuit, giving one measured number y.
Continued fractions
Finds the simplest fraction close to a decimal: 0.834 → 5/6.
LCM
Least common multiple: the smallest number both divide. LCM(2, 3) = 6.
TV distance
½ Σ |P_ours(y) − P_ideal(y)|: 0 means identical, 1 means nothing in common.
Compiled Shor
A circuit built using the answer. Forbidden: it voids Part A.
Logical qubit, code distance d
Many physical qubits protecting one reliable qubit; a larger d means fewer errors and more qubits.

7 · Predict a new case

The judges may change one thing and ask what happens. Our answers, each checked by running the code:

  • N = 15, a = 7?4 bits, so 8 counting qubits and 12 in total. r = 4, peaks at 0, 64, 128, 192. x = 7² mod 15 = 4; gcd(3, 15) = 3 and gcd(5, 15) = 5, so (3, 5).
  • a = 4 for N = 21?4, 16, 1: r = 3 is odd, so factor retries with another a.
  • a = 5 for N = 21?r = 6, but 5³ mod 21 = 20 = −1, so only 1 and 21 come out: retry.
  • N = 25?A perfect power (5²): answered classically as (5, 5), no circuit. The quantum reduction can't work for powers of a prime.
  • Read the counting bits in reverse order?TV 0.542 for (21, 2): the judges' test fails. It's one of our negative controls.
  • Forget to set the calculator to 1?TV 0.833 for (21, 2): it stays 0, so there is no pattern to find.
  • Feed random numbers instead of the circuit's output?factor succeeds 0 of 100 times, thanks to the half-of-the-clues check.

Say it to the jury

  1. Our circuit never sees the period: it is built from N and a only, and factor() fails when you feed it random numbers instead of the circuit's output.
  2. The quantum part finds the period; continued fractions and a gcd turn the period into factors.
  3. What a control test showed us: with the half-of-the-clues check switched off, factor() "factored" pure random numbers 70 times out of 70, so we keep that check.
  4. RSA-2048 needs about a million noisy qubits by the best estimate; today's best machines are thousands of times smaller, but the requirement keeps falling.
